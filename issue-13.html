From Field to Table: bacon-wrapped dove poppers

Everybody argues about how to cook a dove. This is the recipe that ends the argument. It is the classic tailgate popper, and it is the reason half the people at the field edge are not really there for the shooting. Breast your birds out (two clean fillets off the breastbone per dove, no plucking required), and you are most of the way there.

Doneness and safety note: dove breast is dark, lean meat that eats best hot and fast with a warm pink center, the same principle behind that backstrap in Issue #8. Cook it past that and it turns dry and livery, which is where doves get their bad reputation. If you would rather cook birds to a fully done, no-pink center, drop the poppers into a covered braise instead and let them go low and slow. Two housekeeping rules that matter more in September heat than any recipe: wear nitrile gloves when you clean birds and wash up after, and get your doves onto ice in the field, not on a hot tailgate — the same cool-fast rule we hammered back in Issue #7.

Gear Check: shells up front, birds cooling in back

On the opener you stand a field edge for hours in the heat, and a good vest earns its keep three ways: shells within reach, a spot for your license, water, and phone, and a mesh game bag that keeps your birds off your sweaty back. The mesh is the point. It breathes while you shoot and drains and airs your birds while you walk, which buys you time before they hit the cooler.

The value benchmark is the MidwayUSA Dove Vest: a prewashed cotton-poly twill body with mesh front, sides, and back, external shell loops that hold their shape, roomy cargo pockets, and a large blood-proof game bag. It runs around $30 to $40, so check current pricing and stock.

Floor option: a simple dove belt or shell pouch with a mesh game bag, around $20 to $30. Step up: an ALPS OutdoorZ Upland Game X 2.0 or a Beretta upland vest, which rides the load on your hips for three to four times the money. Conditions: heat and fire — read this before you head out

Good news on the fire map heading into the opener: the two incidents we have been tracking in our country are winding down. The Bug Fire on the Lassen and Sierra county line sits around 93,700 acres and roughly 97 percent contained, deep into mop-up, and the MP18 Fire near Hoopa in Humboldt County is around 7,600 acres and about three-quarters contained, with evacuations rolled back to warnings. Do not read that as all-clear, though. The nation is still at Preparedness Level 5, the highest there is, which means firefighting crews and aircraft are stretched thin and a new start today will not draw the same fast response it would have in June. CAL FIRE's Northern California outlook calls for warmer-than-normal inland temperatures and occasional thunderstorms into September, and that thunder is the real risk: dry lightning on cured grass is how the next big one begins.
